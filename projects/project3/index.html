Part A: Image Warping and Mosaicing

Overview

In this project, I used multiple photographs to create image mosaics by registering, projective warping, and resampling them. I computed homographies to warp images. Later in part B, I learned to create algorithms like ANMS and RANSAC which are applied to creating mosiacs automatically as opposed to hand selecting points.

Part A.2: Homography Calculation

To capture photos that blend well, we require transforms between them are projective (a.k.a. perspective) by fixing the center of projection and rotating my camera while capturing photos.

A homography is a projective transformation between two planes

The transformation can be shown by H, a 3x3 matrix H:

$$ H \cdot p_{\text{original}} = p_{\text{transformed}} $$ $$ \begin{bmatrix} h_1 & h_2 & h_3 \\ h_4 & h_5 & h_6 \\ h_7 & h_8 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} wx' \\ wy' \\ w \end{bmatrix} $$

We can solve for H by solving a system of equations with eight degrees of freedom, using the keypoints we generated using this tool. Full set of points:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} x_1 & y_1 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -x'_1 x_1 & -x'_1 y_1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & x_1 & y_1 & 1 & -y'_1 x_1 & -y'_1 y_1 \\ x_2 & y_2 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -x'_2 x_2 & -x'_2 y_2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & x_2 & y_2 & 1 & -y'_2 x_2 & -y'_2 y_2 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots \\ x_n & y_n & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -x'_n x_n & -x'_n y_n \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & x_n & y_n & 1 & -y'_n x_n & -y'_n y_n \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} h_1 \\ h_2 \\ h_3 \\ h_4 \\ h_5 \\ h_6 \\ h_7 \\ h_8 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x'_1 \\ y'_1 \\ x'_2 \\ y'_2 \\ \vdots \\ x'_n \\ y'_n \end{bmatrix} $$

We solve this with a least-squares to find the minimum norm solution with more than four points. We want more points than the min 4 in order to make our homography recovery more robust against noise.

Implementation: We use A matrix and b vector with np.zeros. There are 8 unknown h vals. We loop through the n point Correspondences from the first image and the second image, let us denote this as x,y and x',y' respectivley. For each pair we create two rows, one for x' and one for y' that correspond to the equation from above. Then, we use np.linalg.lstsq to sove our system. It finds the h vector with 8 elements and gives the best possible fit/least sqs soln. Then we create a 3x3 H matrix like we saw in class. H[2, 2]is set to 0, and the other 8 points are taken from h_vector.

Part A.3: Image Warping & Rectification

I implemented a warping function that takes the source image and the homography matrix H corresponding to the transformation from the source to the target image. For each point in the polygon, I reverse interpolate from the source image by applying H^-1 to recover the corresponding point in the source images.

Implementation: Both interpolate methods use invrse warping. We create a grid of al pixel cords for the output image, then apply the inverse Homography to the grid.

For NN, we find the closest pixel in x and y direction by using np.round to int. We create a mask to ensure te pixel coords are inside the bounds of the original image. Then we assign colors to pixels that are within bounds.

For Bilinear, I used the example of corners represnting pixels. I used floor to get this pixel corner in all four directions. Then to find weights we need to take the fractional distances that tell us how far into the cell the point is. Based on thsi the weight for each corner is the area of opposite rectangle. The corner that the pixel is nearest to is given the heighest weight. Then, to get the colors, we interpolate with our weighted averages multipled by the 4 corner colors. Then we assign thees colors to our output image.

In terms of time it took for the functions to run, warpImageNearestNeighbor took between 0.2-0.3s on average across the runs I did. warpImageBilinear took 0.6s. Despite warpImageBilinear taking longer, it resulted in smoother imagesan NN. With NN we experience aliasing since it takes on the closest pixel, whereas with Bilinear we take the weighted avg of four nearest pixels with blurs hard edges. Bilinear takes longer run since it has to compute weighted averages across four pixels, but the image quality is better.

Part A.4: Blend the Images into a Mosaic

Here, I try to stitch 2-3 images into one mosaic. I calculate the homography, warp the images to align, then blend together with weigthed averaging. I reused compute H which calculates 3x3 homography matrix H by taking in two lists of matching points between im1 and im2. I used the Correspondence tool given from class to get these points. We need at least 4 pairs. Then, I used bilinear interpolation to get the best colors per pixel with weighted averaging. This blends the colors to get a smoother image. Then, we use the image, homography H, and target size to compute a transformed image with inverse warping. We create a blank grid, get the inversehomography for H, then multiply the grid by the inverse homography to get source coords for every pixel which maps the pixels from the original image to the output image. We loop through every pixel in the output image and get the corresponding source coordinate, use bilinear interpolation to get the blended collor and place it into the output image.

We need to stitch the images together with a function to create mosiacs. We first create a translation matrix T that shifts to ensure everything will fit on the grid. To blend images at the overlaps, we can use distance_transform_edt to get a weight mask and feather these edges. Then, for each image, we warp it onto the main grid with T @ H and warp the feathered mask. We aslo keep track of the pixel colors' weighted averages and the sum of the weights, which we can divide to get a weighted average for all the pixels.

In the 3 image case, I had to use the Correspondence tool to calculate homographies. Then I chained the matrices like so: H_3_to_1 = H_2_to_1 @ H_3_to_2 and this allowed the 3 images to warp for my mosaic.

Part B: Automatic Image Stitching

In this part of the project, I implemented an automatic mosaicing pipeline based on the paper "Multi-Image Matching using Multi-Scale Oriented Patches" by Brown et al. This involved automatically detecting features, describing them, matching them, and using a robust algorithm (RANSAC) to find the correct homography.

B.1: Harris Corner Detection and ANMS

I first used the provided starter code Harris corner detector to find interest points in the image. This resulted in many clustered points. To get a more spatially distributed set of features, I implemented Adaptive Non-Maximal Suppression (ANMS). For each point, ANMS finds the minimum radius to the nearest neighbor that has a stronger corner response, and I keep the points with the largest radii.

I used the paper to develop ANMS. Every corner gets a suppression radius. It loops through every single corner. If no other corners have a higher harris score, tehn the radius is set to inf. If there are stronger corners, it picks the closest corner and the distance between is the suppression radius. After the loop all corners have a radius and corners that have no strong corners nearby will have a large radius. We then sort corners by their raidus and pick the top 500.

B.2: Feature Descriptor Extraction

Following Section 4 of the paper, I extracted a feature descriptor for each point from ANMS. I first sampled a 40x40 patch, blurred it, and downsampled it to an 8x8 patch. This blurring and downsampling makes the descriptor more robust to small shifts. Finally, I normalized the 8x8 patch to be zero mean and unit variance.

I created extract_descriptors to take a list of corner coords. For each corner, we extract a 40x40 window which has a Gaussian blur applied to reduce noise. This makes the descriptor capture more blocky shapes by reducing noise. Then I subsample to a 8x8 patch. Finally I normalize by taking the mean brightness adn standard deviation.

B.3: Feature Matching

I implemented the matching strategy from the paper. For each feature descriptor in the first image, I found its first and second nearest neighbors in the second image using Euclidean distance. I used Lowe's ratio test: a match was accepted only if the ratio (distance to 1st NN) / (distance to 2nd NN) was below a threshold. I chose a threshold of 0.7 based on figure 6b. This whole process requires calling get_harris_corners, filtering for the top 500 with anms, calling extract_descriptors, then returning the final 500 coords and 500 descriptrs.

B.4: RANSAC [RANdom SAmple Consensus] for Robust Homography

As we can see, the feature matches from B.3 still contained outliers. I implemented RANSAC. This part will allow us to automatically find and visualize matching points between our different images - no more manually clicking, yay!
1. Randomly select 4 feature pairs.
2. Compute the exact homography H from these 4 pairs.
3. Transform all feature points from image 1 using H.
4. Count the number of "inliers": points whose transformed location is within a threshold of their matched point in image 2.
5. After all iterations, keep the homography that had the largest set of inliers and re-compute H using all inliers from this set to get a more stable result.

My final algorithm involves all the functions we've defined throughout this project. Before, we got the harris corners, use ANMS, and extract the descriptors on each image. Now we want to compare them, and we can do this with match_features to get the top 500 features and descriptors, then get the best matches. However, we saw there were outliers. RANSAC comes in to solve this issue with the algorithm above. We call ransac on the result of match_features between 2 images and the two coordinate sets between those same two images. RANSAC then outputs the homography matrix H and the list of best inliers for that homography. If we want to mosaic together more than 2 images, we must run RANSAC on each adjacent images pair. Finally, we use the create_mosaic(images, homographies_ransac) from part A and pass in the list of images and homographies_ransac which is the best H for each pair.
